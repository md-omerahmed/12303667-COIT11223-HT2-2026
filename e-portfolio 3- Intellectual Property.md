# A brief description of the artefact
This video is an interview between LegalVision and IP Australia on how an Australian business takes an idea from a concept to commercialisation while also protecting the idea. It deals with the various types of rights that can be registered in Australia, and also explains that each is concerned with a different element of a product – a trademark protects the brand image, a design protects how a product looks and a patent protects how a product works. It repeatedly emphasises that registration is not automatic (as with copyright) and that companies should consider protection of their IP well before a product comes to market, before another company has copied it.

# Explanation of the reason for my selection of artefact
I selected this video because the content builds on that of the workshop on ‘Foundations of Intellectual Property' on which the students attended, where we learned about patents, trademarks and designs all being administered by IP Australia and requiring their own registration. This was much more understandable to me than the slides of the workshop when I heard it in the context of a real example of commercialisation. It was also convenient that the video was Australian and contemporary, as IP law varies from jurisdiction to jurisdiction and I wanted something to be more relevant to my context than an explainer from the United States.

# Detailed summary of the artefac
The government has put its foot down on the introduction of a copyright exemption for AI companies training models using Australian creative works, says Truu (2025). A plan to allow AI developers to copy creative work from others without permission proposed in a Productivity Commission report and was heavily criticized by musicians, authors and others. The Attorney-General said the government would be unlikely to dilute the copyright protections and a working group is still looking at both the licensing and enforcement issues involved in ensuring creators are paid for when their work is used to train artificial intelligence systems.

# Justification on why I chose the artefact
This article has been selected as it illustrates how AI is being tested in Australian copyright law in real time and relates closely to the workshop topic of copyright creep and who has the responsibility to enforce IP rights. It made me feel like copyright is not some legal term I learned about in a book, it's something that's happening now in my country between the government, tech companies and individual artists. I also appreciated its discussion of both sides of the argument, the economic argument for the training data for AI, and the creators' argument for having control over it, which gave me food for thought and a sense of compromise between innovation and protection that was described in the workshop.

# Artefact 3: Scholarly Article — Copyright law in the age of AI.
Copyright law in the age of AI: analysing the AI-generated works and copyright challenges in Australia, Thambaiya, N, International Review of Law, Computers & Technology, 2025, vol. 39, no. 3 448–473
<img width="2842" height="1444" alt="image" src="https://github.com/user-attachments/assets/62bc1634-4dad-4b12-a486-5ff88a3c9796" />
https://doi.org/10.1080/13600869.2025.2486893

# Brief description of the artefact
This article explores the implications of the AI-generated works under Australian copyright law. As explained by Thambaiya (2025, p. 449), only works of human authorship are protected under the Copyright Act 1968 (Cth), meaning that it is not clear whether a work created independently by an AI system is protected by copyright. It contrasts this with overseas models and reviews the pro and con arguments for and against extending copyright to AI-generated works, before making a recommendation for legislative reform to ensure Australian law 'catches up' with the technology.

# Justification on why I chose the artefact
I selected this article due to the direct relevance of the case of the Monkey Selfie and the question for discussion in the workshop: "Who owns pictures generated by AI? I read a peer-reviewed source, and I realized that the concept of “human authorship” I had been introduced to at the workshop is not a new issue, but rather the same legal concept that generative AI is testing. I used Google Scholar to search for “AI copyright Australia”, which found this article, and picked one from 2025 to be current and local.
